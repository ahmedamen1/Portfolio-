Credit Approval Prediction

Classifying loan applicants as good or bad credit risks from demographic records and monthly repayment history. The dataset has no label column, so the first real task was deciding what "bad" means, and only 3.4% of clients end up on that side of the line.

What it does

The interesting work was upstream of the model. The data arrives as two tables and no answer key.

Builds the labels from repayment historyApplicant records and monthly credit records join on client ID. Each month carries a status code, which I scored and summed per client, then thresholded into good and bad.
Engineers a weighted credit scoreNot every late month means the same thing, so statuses are weighted rather than counted, giving one composite weighted_points feature per client.
Corrects a 29:1 class imbalance40,048 good clients against 1,401 bad. SMOTE synthesises minority examples on the training split so the model sees a balanced problem.
Prunes features that carry nothingDropped zero-importance columns, including a mobile-phone flag that was constant across the dataset, using XGBoost feature importances.
Compares six model familiesLogistic regression, k-nearest neighbours, SVM, decision tree, random forest and XGBoost, each on the same split.
Tunes the finalistGrid search with cross-validation over depth, learning rate, estimator count, subsample ratio and minimum child weight.

Three problems worth the write-up

On a dataset that is 96.6% one class, the headline metric is the thing most likely to mislead you.

The dataset had no target variableNeither table says whether a client is creditworthy — just applicants and monthly status codes. I weighted the codes by severity, summed them per client, and thresholded. That threshold is a modelling choice, not ground truth: move it and both the class balance and every score on this page move with it.
Why 95.6% accuracy is not the achievementOnly 1,401 of 41,449 clients are bad, so predicting "good" for everyone scores about 96.6% while learning nothing. Accuracy cannot separate a working model from that constant, so the comparison worth reporting is against a real learner: logistic regression managed 58.5% on balanced data against XGBoost's 95.6%. SMOTE stops the training distribution rewarding the lazy answer, and the per-class precision and recall are what I actually read.
Picking the model that was not the top scorerRandom forest beat the tuned XGBoost on raw accuracy, 96.0% to 95.6%. I chose XGBoost anyway, on per-class precision and recall and on interpretability — its feature importances were already driving the pruning step. A four-tenths gap on an imbalanced dataset is noise, and picking on it would be fitting the decision to the test split.

What I am not claiming

This is university coursework for Machine Learning (CIE 417) at Zewail City, supervised by Dr. Omar Fahmy, not a deployed credit system. It was graded A-.

The labels are constructed, not observed. Everything downstream inherits the threshold I chose, so the scores describe performance against my own definition of a bad client rather than against real defaults.

SMOTE was used to balance training data. It synthesises minority examples rather than finding new ones, which helps a model learn but does not add real information about rare cases.
